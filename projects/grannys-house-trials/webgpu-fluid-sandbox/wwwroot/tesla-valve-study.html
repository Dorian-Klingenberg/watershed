A Tesla valve should bias flow, not seal it

A Tesla valve is a fixed, passive conduit. Its asymmetric side loops let flow pass in both directions, while the preferred direction should encounter less hydraulic resistance. Reverse flow is repeatedly diverted into loops and interacts with returning flow, increasing losses. It is a fluidic diode, not a one-way check valve.

Proposed trial geometry

First candidate: three compact repeated loops, a four-cell-wide main run, roughly 18-cell-long branches, and a 30° downstream merge. Keep a 45° merge as the comparison case. The 30° candidate gave the strongest easy-versus-hard main-channel pulse-transfer split among the tested 30°, 45°, and 60° versions.

What was compared

The trials used the exact C# staggered-MAC model from this sandbox, with first-order velocity advection enabled. The current terrain is a broad single bypass beside an eight-cell-wide straight run. Candidate layouts reduced the run to four cells, scaled each bypass down, and repeated the bypass three times. For each direction, twenty one-inch drops were added at 24-tick intervals at the inlet, for 100 cell-inches total, then the model advanced to tick 2400. “Center crossing” is the cumulative net volume crossing a full-width section at the middle of the main run, expressed in cell-inches. Branch storage is the final water held in the bypasses.

For the pulse setup, left-to-right is called the intended easy direction: it meets the narrow branch neck before the wide scoop. Right-to-left meets the wide scoop first and is the intended harder direction. Results are transient comparisons from the same initially dry state. The far reservoir remained dry, so center crossing is not a completed source-to-reservoir throughput measurement.

Orientation and model controls

Mirroring the current single loop made main-channel pulse transfer nearly symmetric: 6.123 versus 6.153 cell-inches. The current layout also changes apparent preference with the test setup: its fixed-head equal-tick sample at tick 1600 favors forward discharge by about 10%, while the empty-start small-drop pulse moves 78% more water through the center in reverse. That mismatch is why the existing single-loop result should not be treated as dependable directional rectification.

With velocity advection disabled, the current layout crossed 5.301 cell-inches forward and 10.017 reverse in the same pulse protocol. This model’s response depends on transient storage and momentum treatment as well as the drawing.

Physics and limits

Real Tesla-valve action comes from direction-dependent losses in an asymmetric, fixed passage. In the favorable direction, most flow can continue down the main path, with limited diversion. In reverse, the branch entrances capture more of the stream; turns, contractions, separation, recirculation and collisions with returning flow dissipate more energy. The geometry permits both directions. Performance changes with Reynolds number and flow rate, so a small, slow drop experiment may show weak or inconsistent bias.

A meaningful engineering measure is diodicity: pressure drop in the unfavorable direction divided by pressure drop in the favorable direction at matched flow rate. This sandbox instead reports finite-time water transfer and branch storage. Its two-dimensional, depth-averaged MAC scheme does not resolve three-dimensional boundary layers, detailed vortex structure, or turbulent losses; one grid cell is one foot. Treat these runs as a way to screen candidate shapes, then measure matched-flow pressure drop in a higher-fidelity fluid solver or physical model.
