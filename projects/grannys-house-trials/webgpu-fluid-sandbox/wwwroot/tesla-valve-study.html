Patent plan trace · staggered MAC playground

Stamp the patent geometry into terrain

Both Tesla presets now load one terrain traced from the plan view in Fig. 1 of Tesla's US1329559A patent. This replaces the earlier hand-drawn D/U-shaped loops. The stamped map keeps the alternating slanted partitions and hooked side clearances that make the real device asymmetric, while leaving the app's direct central route open.

Current top-down cell map

Why the grid is larger: the patent body has a long, shallow planform. A uniform stamp at 96 × 64 collapsed most angled partitions into horizontal strips. The 192 × 128 terrain preserves those angles with a 16-cell body width and about 172 cells of length. At the app's 1-ft cells this is 16 ft across; retaining a 4-ft physical width would require quarter-foot cells and corresponding cell-area changes throughout the solvers. Some one-foot raster gaps still close, so the map generator records and reopens the smallest connecting throats.

What the patent geometry is meant to do

The patent describes a fixed passage that carries flow in both directions. In its preferred direction, the main stream proceeds nearly straight with small deflections. With the pressure reversed, the buckets and partitions redirect the stream repeatedly, increasing turns and collisions with returning flow. Those extra losses create a directional pressure-drop difference; they do not make a perfect check valve. A flow arrow in the drawing is not a wall or a guaranteed streamline.

The app's first preset runs a repeatable forward/reverse head comparison. The second uses the same map for small pours and right-click springs. Try Staggered MAC with velocity advection enabled and place sources at (55, 60) and (67, 67) to explore an upper and lower side passage. The left-port pour begins at (8, 64).

First controlled result

With the channel initially wetted, equal 18/12 in reservoir levels, Staggered MAC and upwind velocity advection, the tick-1600 main-section reading was 3.0691 ft³/s left-to-right and −2.9196 ft³/s right-to-left. The off-axis section read 3.5134 and −4.0813 ft³/s. Both directions carried water; the reverse trial had greater off-axis movement in that sample.

These are last-tick transient transfers, not a settled equal-flow pressure-drop comparison. They do not establish a diodicity ratio. The solver is depth-averaged and does not resolve 3D boundary layers, separated jets or turbulent mixing. The result is useful for exploring this raster, not a claim of physical validation.
